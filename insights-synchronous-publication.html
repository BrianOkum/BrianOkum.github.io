Synchronous Publication & Planned Periodic Review

Field notes on document control

It’s the difference between chaos and organization at every stage. I’ve been part of development of multiple review/publication processes, so I’ve seen what works and what doesn’t.

But What Happens When This… Becomes This?

So what happens when one document holds up the entire package? It happens. Sometimes you’re handed an impossible timeline and all that matters is getting the SOP and compliance docs ready. Other times it’s completely out of anyone’s control; for example, a chemical manufacturer can’t deliver on a Safety Data Sheet, which means your nearly complete batch record that the QC team needs to hit the ground running is missing some specs for safety testing. Whatever the situation, what do you do when some of those documents need to be published ASAP, but that one just isn’t ready?

Iterative Improvement

This is where the model needs to become flexible, not rigid, and where the philosophy of iterative improvement comes into play. There are different ways to handle it depending on circumstances. If regulatory standards aren’t a consideration, one way is to publish the first major version and notate the batch record that that missing specification is pending receival of third-party documents and will be included in v2.0.

If there’s zero tolerance in regs, then be flexible with the model. Break the entire document change control into two, where one gets published and the other sits until the trouble document is fixed. Synchronous publication is a best practice and a model of how things ideally play out. In my experience, there is a lot more variation in how things actually happen though, and any model or ‘best practice’ that has zero flexibility as a policy is bound to cause friction.

Iterative improvement is a manufacturing philosophy that applies equally to technical documentation. Perfection is something to strive for, yet ‘perfect’ should not become the enemy of ‘good’. There’s no shame in releasing v1.0 and immediately initiating a change control for v1.1 (that’s barring strict regulatory standards of course, but then it wouldn’t be shame but rather penalties). In fact, there are known benefits to this method. The sooner the end-user can provide real feedback, the sooner a superior v2.0 becomes a reality.
